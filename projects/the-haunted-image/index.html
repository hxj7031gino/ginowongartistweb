The Haunted Image

通过对丝网印刷工艺的持续改良，将印刷颜料替换成镜面颜料印刷在透胶片上。我试图在这些作品抵抗一切数字化尝试的过程中，重新建立图像所承载的信息与观众之间的关系。在当今社会，图像作为最基本的信息交换媒介，早已被大量生产、过度编辑、扭曲，并被人工智能生成内容所充斥。图像所传递的信息也因此被景观化，并逐渐失去其原本的真实感。观众将无法成功拍摄这些作品；他们最终得到的，只会是一张自己正拿着手机或相机拍照的影像。通过干扰数字镜头，我试图召回那种被幽灵般遮蔽的真实，并重新连接每一个个体与其自身所面对的现实。

对于熟悉左派理论的读者来说，情境主义国际及其核心观念“景观社会”，是20世纪资本主义批判运动中不可忽视的力量。不同于同时代活跃的法兰克福学派，情境主义国际借用亨利·列斐伏尔的《日常生活批判》，将批判场域从工厂和工会转向日常生活，并通过城市游走、海报标语和事件制造，介入被资本塑造的现实。

法国五月风暴之后，组织迅速萎缩，并在四年后解散。他们及其理论本身，也像其他批判资本主义的先驱一样，被系统吸纳，成为景观的一部分。经过互联网科技革命后，情境主义国际当年的批判，在今天这个流媒体成为主要信息来源的时代仍然得以延续。虚拟空间早已成为日常生活的一部分，现实的意义仍然不来自生活关系，而是越来越多地产生于图像和视频制造出来的景观之中。

基于这种对日常生活被图像全面殖民的反思，我选择将那些在艺术史上被极端景观化、过度消费的古典母题进行再现。这些世界名画的原作语境已被剥离，并在流媒体与大众文化工业的合谋下，被降维成社交媒体上的消费背景、无限复制的衍生品，以及虚拟空间里泛滥的数字像素。观众不再注视作品本身，而是注视着“关于作品的数字景观”及其所代表的文化权威。

因此，我将这个系列命名为《图像的幽灵》。我使用镜面颜料改良丝网印刷工艺，并将其印刷在透明胶片上，使镜面反射像素取代CMYK，成为组成图像的最小单元。最终，现场的观众和空间也会反射在镜面像素之中。在此显现的人与空间，才真正构成了图像意义的一部分，而非任何为其立法的文化权威。此时，所有试图用镜头将图像数据化的尝试，最终都只会剩下手拿摄影设备的观众。镜面像素并非对数字设备的排斥，而是直接利用镜头的自动对焦与曝光机制。数字镜头的技术逻辑与观众的物理肉身同时在镜面上留下影像；这两者不再是对立的，而是共同成为材料，构成图像最终的呈现状态。

Through the continuous refinement of the screen-printing process, I replace conventional printing pigments with mirror-like pigments and print them onto acetate film. In these works, as they resist every attempt at digital capture, I seek to re-establish the relationship between the information carried by the image and the viewer. In contemporary society, the image, as the most fundamental medium of information exchange, has long been mass-produced, excessively edited, distorted, and saturated by AI-generated content. As a result, the information conveyed by images has itself become spectacularised, gradually losing its original sense of reality. Viewers will be unable to successfully photograph these works; what they ultimately obtain is merely an image of themselves holding a phone or camera in the act of taking a picture. By interfering with the digital lens, I attempt to summon back a reality that has been ghostly obscured, and to reconnect each individual with the reality that confronts them.

For readers familiar with leftist theory, the Situationist International and its core concept of “the society of the spectacle” remain an indispensable force within twentieth-century critiques of capitalism. Unlike the Frankfurt School, which was active during the same period, the Situationist International drew upon Henri Lefebvre’s Critique of Everyday Life, shifting the site of critique from the factory and the trade union to everyday life itself. Through urban drifting, posters, slogans, and the production of events, they intervened in a reality shaped by capital.

After the events of May 1968 in France, the organisation rapidly declined and dissolved four years later. Like many other pioneers of anti-capitalist critique, they and their theories were eventually absorbed by the very system they had sought to oppose, becoming part of the spectacle itself. Following the technological revolution of the internet, the critique advanced by the Situationist International continues to resonate in the present age, in which streaming media has become a primary source of information. Virtual space has long since become part of everyday life, while the meaning of reality is increasingly produced not through lived social relations, but through the spectacles manufactured by images and videos.

Based on this reflection on the comprehensive colonisation of everyday life by images, I choose to reproduce classical motifs that have been excessively spectacularised and over-consumed within art history. The original contexts of these world-famous paintings have been stripped away; through the collusion of streaming media and the mass cultural industry, they have been reduced to consumable backgrounds on social media, endlessly reproduced derivatives, and proliferating digital pixels within virtual space. Viewers no longer look at the work itself, but at the “digital spectacle about the work” and the cultural authority it represents.

For this reason, I have titled the series The Haunted Image. I use mirror-like pigments to modify the screen-printing process and print them onto transparent acetate film, allowing reflective mirror pixels to replace CMYK as the smallest units that constitute the image. Ultimately, the viewers and the surrounding space are also reflected within these mirror pixels. The people and the space that appear in this moment are what truly become part of the image’s meaning, rather than any cultural authority that seeks to legislate that meaning. At this point, every attempt to transform the image into data through the camera lens ultimately leaves behind only the viewer holding a photographic device. These mirror pixels do not reject digital devices; instead, they directly make use of the camera’s mechanisms of autofocus and exposure. The technological logic of the digital lens and the viewer’s physical body are simultaneously inscribed upon the mirrored surface. Rather than remaining opposed to one another, they become materials together, constituting the final state in which the image appears.
